## Liner Notes

Two months into lockdown, and the mixes are getting restless. Ninety tracks in two hours and twenty minutes — the highest track-per-minute ratio the archive has produced. Where the early marathons spread everything out across three-plus hours, Mix 08 compresses. Ideas arrive faster, transitions happen quicker, selections flash past before you've fully absorbed them. It's the mixing equivalent of pacing a room — restless energy contained in a smaller space, a mind that can't sit still because the world outside has forced everything else to stop.

CamelPhat & ARTBAT's "For a Feeling" at 6:45 announces a new sonic direction within the first ten minutes. ARTBAT represent the melodic techno movement that was reshaping club culture in 2020 — driving, emotional, built for vast concrete spaces that nobody could visit. Their collaboration with CamelPhat bridges the UK house sound the archive has been absorbing with the European techno that's been creeping in from the edges. Gorgon City's "Nobody" follows at 12:30, their second appearance in consecutive mixes, confirming that the UK bass-house thread opened in Mix 07 is here to stay.

The middle section contains a burst of pure eclecticism that could only happen in a pandemic. Between the forty-seven and fifty-one minute marks: Robbie Rivera's trance, Armin van Buuren's "Dayglow," Robert Miles' "Children" — the original, not Tinlicker's rework, the 1996 version itself — Clean Bandit's "Symphony," Todd Terje's "Inspector Norse," The Chainsmokers' "#SELFIE," Four Tet's "School," and Caribou's "Never Come Back" in Four Tet's remix. In four minutes, the mix cycles through trance, classical crossover, pop, indie dance, art-house electronics, and intentionally silly EDM. It makes no commercial sense and perfect emotional sense. This is someone alone with their record collection, finding comfort in every corner of it. The "#SELFIE" inclusion is particularly telling — dropped without irony between electronic art pieces, because the DJ hasn't lost their sense of humour and has no one to perform seriousness for.

Toni Braxton's "Un-Break My Heart" appears three times — the Frankie Knuckles Franktidrama Club Mix at 1:14:45, the Classic Radio Mix at 1:16:00, and the Frankie Knuckles version again at 1:20:15. Three readings of the same song clustered around the seventy-five minute mark. This is Frankie Knuckles — the godfather of house music — reworking one of the '90s' defining R&B ballads, and the triple appearance reads less as an accident than as someone unable to move past a particular feeling. The track sits at the emotional centre of the mix like a held breath. In May 2020, "un-break my heart" carries a weight the songwriter never intended.

Paperclip People's "Throw" in Slam's RTM Remix makes its debut at 1:40:30 — a pivotal discovery. This is Carl Craig's Detroit techno alias, and the track is a masterclass in percussive tension and release, all rolling hi-hats and hypnotic synth stabs. It will return in six more mixes over the next five years, always deployed at moments of high energy, always functioning as a pressure valve. Its arrival here, deep in the second hour of a pandemic session, feels like the DJ finding a new essential tool — a track they didn't know they needed until they played it.

Fred again.. resurfaces at 58:15 with "Danielle (smile on my face)" — the same track from Mix 03, returning after a one-mix absence. It's followed immediately by KH's "Only Human" at 58:30. These two tracks in sequence represent the cutting edge of UK electronic music in 2020: emotionally raw, sample-driven, built from the textures of everyday life rather than studio perfection. Fade's "All I Got" appears twice — at 20:30 and 1:08:15 — continuing its role as the progressive house anchor that appears whenever the mix needs grounding. Rui Da Silva's "Touch Me" at 2:02:30 introduces another classic that will recur through the archive.

The energy arc dips in its second quarter before rebuilding to its strongest intensity in the closing section. After the Toni Braxton emotional centre, the mix climbs through Bicep's "Atlas" at 1:26:45 — textured, propulsive, gorgeous — then Bonnie Tyler's "Total Eclipse of the Heart" at 1:32:00 (played twice, because some pop classics demand repetition), and Eric Prydz's "Opus" at 1:33:00, one of progressive house music's most iconic compositions, its slow-building melody unfolding like a cathedral being assembled note by note. Frankie Knuckles' "Your Love" at 1:47:00 connects the archive to house music's Chicago roots.

The descent from that peak is extraordinary. Lane 8 & YOTTO's "I/Y" at 2:03:30, then ambient textures, then deep, minimal techno: Deetron's "Photon" at 2:09:30, Christian Smith's "Motor" at 2:09:45, Robag Wruhme's "Nata Alma" at 2:13:30, and Ion Ludwig's "Classilion" closing at 2:19:00. The mix that began with such restless density ends in near-silence — sparse, meditative, barely there. The journey from ninety tracks of compressed energy to these final minutes of ambient drift is the widest emotional arc the archive has attempted.

Mix 08 is the first pandemic session that sounds like it was made by the pandemic — not as a response to it, but as a product of it. The compression, the eclecticism, the emotional whiplash between Toni Braxton and Paperclip People, the willingness to play "#SELFIE" and Eric Prydz within the same breath — this is music as survival mechanism. A two-hour assertion that joy and depth and silliness and seriousness can coexist in the same recording, the same room, the same person. The world was falling apart. The decks were the one thing that still worked.
