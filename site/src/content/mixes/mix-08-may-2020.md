## Liner Notes

Two months into lockdown, and the mixes are getting denser. Mix 08 packs ninety tracks into two hours and twenty minutes — the highest track-per-minute ratio since the archive began. Where the early marathons spread everything out over three-plus hours, Mix 08 compresses. Ideas arrive faster. Transitions happen quicker. It's the mixing equivalent of pacing a room — restless energy contained in a smaller space.

CamelPhat & ARTBAT's "For a Feeling" opens the serious business at track four, and it announces a new sonic direction. ARTBAT represent the melodic techno movement that was reshaping club music in 2020 — driving, emotional, built for vast spaces that nobody could visit. Their collaboration with CamelPhat bridges the UK house sound that the archive has been absorbing with the European techno that's creeping in from the edges. "For a Feeling" will prove to be more than a track — it's a signpost toward where the next several mixes are heading.

The middle section contains a burst of pure eclecticism that could only happen in a pandemic mix. Robert Miles' "Children" — the original, not Tinlicker's rework — sits between Armin van Buuren, Clean Bandit, Todd Terje's "Inspector Norse," The Chainsmokers' "#SELFIE," and Four Tet. It's a stretch that makes no commercial sense but perfect emotional sense: this is someone cycling through every era of dance music they love, finding comfort in familiarity while the world outside is unfamiliar. The "#SELFIE" inclusion is particularly telling — an intentionally silly EDM track dropped without irony between electronic art pieces. The DJ hasn't lost their sense of humour.

Paperclip People's "Throw" in Slam's RTM Remix makes its first appearance at the 100-minute mark. This is Carl Craig's Detroit techno alias, and the track is a masterclass in percussive tension. It will return in six more mixes, always deployed at high-energy moments, always functioning as a pressure release. Its debut here, deep in the second hour of a pandemic mix, feels like a discovery — the moment the DJ finds a new essential tool.

Toni Braxton's "Un-Break My Heart" appears three times — the Frankie Knuckles club mix, the classic radio version, and the Frankie Knuckles mix again. Three appearances of the same song in different forms within a single mix is unusual even for an archive that embraces repetition. But this is Frankie Knuckles — the godfather of house music — reworking one of the '90s' defining R&B ballads. The track sits at the emotional centre of the mix, around the 75-minute mark, and its triple appearance reads less as an accident and more as someone unable to move past a particular feeling.

Fred again.. resurfaces at fifty-eight minutes with "Danielle (smile on my face)" — the same track from Mix 03, now returning after a one-mix absence. It's followed immediately by KH's "Only Human." These two tracks, appearing in sequence, represent the cutting edge of UK electronic music in 2020: emotionally raw, sample-driven, built from the detritus of everyday life rather than studio perfection. Their placement mid-mix, as a pair, suggests the DJ recognises them as kindred spirits.

The energy arc is closing-strong — the highest intensity comes in the final quarter. After the Toni Braxton emotional centre, the mix rebuilds through Pryda, Bicep's "Atlas," a double helping of Bonnie Tyler's "Total Eclipse of the Heart" (another pop classic dropped without apology), and Eric Prydz's "Opus" — one of the most iconic progressive house tracks ever made. The final section moves through Frankie Knuckles, Lane 8 & YOTTO, and then descends into deep ambient techno: Deetron, Christian Smith, Robag Wruhme, Ion Ludwig. It ends in near-silence, which for a mix that began with such density, feels like arriving somewhere very far from where you started.

Mix 08 is the first pandemic mix that sounds like it was made for the pandemic — not as a response to it, but as a product of it. The compression, the eclecticism, the emotional whiplash between Toni Braxton and Paperclip People, the willingness to play "#SELFIE" and Eric Prydz in the same session — this is music as survival mechanism, a two-hour assertion that joy and depth and silliness and seriousness can coexist. The world was falling apart. The decks were the one thing that still worked.
