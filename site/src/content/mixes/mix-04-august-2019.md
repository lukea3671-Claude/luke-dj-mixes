## Liner Notes

Mix 04 is the last of the true marathons. Three hours and thirty-two minutes, 164 tracks — the highest count in the entire archive. After this, the mixes begin to contract, to edit, to choose. But not yet. This is the final exhale before the DJ learns to breathe differently, and it carries the sprawling, unself-conscious energy of someone still building their record collection in public.

The opening minutes move fast — Hayden James, New Baccara, Radio Slave, Jonas Blue — genres flipping like channels on a television. By eight minutes in, Tinlicker & Robert Miles' "Children" appears, only the second time it has surfaced in the archive. The track carries its own mythology: Robert Miles' original "Children" from 1995 is one of the most recognisable melodies in electronic music history, and Tinlicker's reworking treats it with the reverence it deserves. Its placement this early, almost throwaway in the rush of tracks, suggests it hasn't yet earned its status as a centrepiece. That will come.

The RÜFÜS DU SOL presence shifts here. "Hypnotised" arrives at the twenty-minute mark, followed much later by "Solace" in a Lastlings remix and "Like An Animal" in multiple versions — the original, and YOTTO's remix played twice. The repetition of "Like An Animal" is revealing. When a DJ returns to the same track within a single mix, it's usually because they couldn't get it out of their system. The YOTTO remix adds a different texture, more hypnotic, more patient. This is the first time the archive shows the DJ actively working a track rather than simply playing it.

There's a stretch in the middle — roughly the 55-minute to 90-minute mark — where the mix becomes something unexpected. Oliver Koletzki's "Hypnotized," a Mousse T rework of "Purple Rain," French 79's "Hometown," Gigi D'Agostino's "La Passion," Rick James' "Super Freak" into Block & Crown's "You're a Freak," and then — Fleetwood Mac's "Dreams." The Cranberries' "Dreams" appeared twenty minutes earlier. Two different songs called "Dreams" from two different rock traditions, both dropped into a dance mix with no apparent irony. This is the comfort-rock recontextualisation that defines Mix 04: beloved songs from other genres given new life by being placed alongside house and techno. It's a DJ mixing for a house party, not a club.

The energy arc tells the story of a plateau. Unlike the building shapes that will characterise later mixes, Mix 04 peaks in its third quarter and then gently descends. It doesn't build toward a climax — it sustains and then releases. The energy is steady, domestic, the sonic equivalent of a long Saturday spent with music on in the background, occasionally turning the volume up when something demands it.

The underground thread runs deeper here than in previous mixes. Hackler & Kuch's "Special K," Christian Smith & Wehbba's "Mutate," Shane Fontane's "Rituals" — proper techno selections that sit alongside pop remixes without friction. Mr. Fingers' "Can You Feel It" makes an appearance, one of house music's foundational tracks. Adam Freeland's "We Want Your Soul" paired with Fatboy Slim's "Praise You" nods to the big beat era. The reference points are widening. The DJ isn't just collecting current releases — they're reaching back into dance music history.

The final hour is where the marathon nature becomes most pronounced. The tracks arrive at a breathless pace — sometimes a new record every fifteen to thirty seconds. CamelPhat, Sebastian Ingrosso, Robin Schulz, David Guetta in three consecutive versions of "2U," Prince's "Cream" appearing twice. Then a remarkable sequence: Disclosure's "You & Me" in the Flume remix, followed by The Blaze's "TERRITORY," into Lane 8 & YOTTO's "I/Y" — arguably the highest concentration of quality in the mix, buried deep where only the patient listener will find it.

The very end turns contemplative. Klangkarussell's ambient textures, Jean-Michel Jarre meeting Tangerine Dream, moonbooter's "Impact of Mind" — the mix drifts away from dance music entirely and into something almost cinematic. The Temptations' "I Wish It Would Rain" closes the arc from Motown to Berlin and back again, a reminder that all dance music, at its core, descends from the same impulse to move.

This is the last time a mix will try to contain everything. From Mix 05 onward, the tracklists shrink, the running times tighten, and the selections become more deliberate. Mix 04 is the final survey of the landscape before the DJ decides which direction to walk. It's messy, overstuffed, occasionally brilliant, and ultimately necessary — the kind of excess you need to go through before you can learn what to leave out.
