## Liner Notes

March 2020. The month the world locked its doors. Two hours and twenty-eight minutes, eighty-three tracks, and if you didn't know when this was made, you might not hear the crisis in it at first. But it's there — in the way the mix turns inward, in the way it reaches for warmth rather than spectacle, in the way it closes with two different songs sharing the same name, as though the DJ is reaching through the speakers to make a connection with whoever might be listening alone.

The opening is darker than anything before it. A Tash Sultana remix recast as techno at 4:00, SOFI TUKKER's "Drinkee" at 4:45, a moody Jonny Calypso piece at 8:00. Where previous mixes launched with crowd-ready anthems or cinematic overtures, Mix 07 begins in shadow. House music's share of the palette climbs to its highest point in the archive so far, and when the world shrinks to four walls, the music contracts too — deeper, more interior, more concerned with what happens in a single room than on a festival stage.

Kidnap's "Moments" in the Ben Böhmer & Nils Hoffmann remix at 23:30 is a watershed moment. Ben Böhmer represents a branch of melodic house that prioritises atmosphere over rhythm — music built for headphone listening as much as dancefloors, music that assumes the listener is alone. This is the first time that specific sensibility appears in the archive, and it marks a turning point. The selections are no longer designed for a crowd. They're designed for solitude — which is exactly what March 2020 demanded.

Andy Compton's "That Acid Track" arrives at 42:00, right on schedule — the deep house metronome that has been ticking since Mix 02, now as reliable as a heartbeat. Solee's "Shanti" follows at 1:13:00, equally dependable, equally calming. These recurring tracks aren't just favourites; they're structural elements, the way a novelist uses recurring motifs. Repetition across time creates a kind of meaning that novelty cannot. You hear "That Acid Track" and you know where you are, no matter how far the mix has wandered.

The forty-five-minute mark contains perhaps the most extraordinary selection in the archive to this point. Leonard Cohen's "You Want It Darker" in Paul Kalkbrenner's remix. Cohen's final album, released just weeks before his death in 2016, reimagined as Berlin techno. The original is about mortality and faith — "Hineni, hineni, I'm ready, my Lord." The remix strips those themes to their rhythmic bones and rebuilds them for the dancefloor. In March 2020, with a pandemic beginning to reshape every aspect of ordinary life, the choice feels prescient in a way the DJ almost certainly didn't intend. Some selections are ahead of their moment.

Gorgon City enter the archive for the first time at 1:16:00 with "Go All Night" featuring Jennifer Hudson in a Nicky Night Time remix. Their brand of UK bass-inflected house — grittier, more vocal, more indebted to garage than the European melodic sound that has dominated — brings a different texture entirely. They'll become a staple, returning in eight mixes through Mix 40. Four Tet's "Baby" at 1:08:15 and Stardust's "Music Sounds Better with You" at 1:01:15 — one of house music's most perfect records — anchor the middle section with a quality that transcends any era.

The energy arc builds early and then gradually, gently descends — the second quarter carries the highest intensity, and the mix spends its remaining time coming back down. It's the architecture of a set designed for a specific emotional journey: draw the listener in, deliver the peak when they're most receptive, then bring them home slowly. Dom Dolla's "San Frandisco" at 1:29:30, Fade's "All I Got" at 1:05:15, Route 94's "My Love" at 2:05:30 — these tracks sustain warmth during the long, gradual release. It's a more sophisticated structure than anything in the marathon era.

The closing stretch is the most emotionally articulate ending the archive has produced. London Grammar's "Darling Are You Gonna Leave Me" at 1:36:15 — brittle, vulnerable, arena-sized. CamelPhat's "Cola" in ZHU's remix at 1:43:30. Lana Del Rey's "Summertime Sadness" at 1:49:45. Dermot Kennedy's "Power Over Me" in MEDUZA's remix at 1:52:15. Deee-Lite's "What Is Love?" at 1:50:30. Each track asks a question the pandemic was forcing everyone to confront. Shouse's "Love Tonight" surfaces at 2:03:15, a track that will become one of the defining dance records of the early 2020s, though in March 2020 it's still just a warm promise.

The final two tracks share a title. Hayden James' "Something About You" in ODESZA's remix at 2:20:15, and Elderbrook & Rudimental's "Something About You" in its chill mix at 2:25:30. Two different songs, same name, played back to back as the mix dissolves into silence. It's either coincidence or instinct, and in the context of a world suddenly defined by isolation, it reads as the latter — a DJ ending a session by repeating, as if in prayer, that there is something about you, whoever you are, wherever you're listening, that matters. This is the first pandemic mix, and it carries all the hallmarks of that moment: heightened emotionality, inward focus, an instinct for human connection. The marathon era is decisively over. What has replaced it is more deliberate, more emotionally intelligent, and more aware that music isn't just entertainment. In March 2020, it was a way of surviving.
