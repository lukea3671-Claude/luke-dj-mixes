## Liner Notes

Fifty-eight minutes. Thirty tracks. The first sub-hour mix in the archive, and the most striking statement of discipline in seventeen chapters. Where Mix 01 needed three hours and forty-five minutes, Mix 17 needs less than one. The marathon era isn't just over — from this vantage point, it's almost inconceivable. The DJ who emerges here understands something the earlier mixes hadn't yet learned: a complete thought doesn't need to fill a room.

House takes the top spot in the genre breakdown for the first time — 38%, overtaking Dance for the first time in the archive's history. South Street Player's "(Who?) Keeps Changing Your Mind?" opens at 0:00, immediately followed by two more house tracks in the first minute. The gradual drift toward house that began in the pandemic era reaches its logical conclusion here. This isn't a revolution — it's an accumulation of instincts that have been sharpening for three years. The DJ's centre of gravity has shifted permanently toward the four-on-the-floor groove.

Bicep's "Glue" returns at 42:30 — the original, not a remix, not a rework. Its last appearance was in Mix 01, where it sat at track three, seven minutes into a three-hour-forty-five-minute marathon. Now it arrives three-quarters of the way through a fifty-eight-minute set. Same track, entirely different context. In Mix 01 it was a discovery — the sound of a door opening. Here, four years later, it's a memory — the sound of looking back at that door from a great distance. The DJ is reaching back to the beginning, and the gesture is both nostalgic and knowing.

Fred again.. bookends the session — "Kahan (Last Year)" featuring Kodak Black at 20:45, and "Dermot (See Yourself In My Eyes)" closing at 54:45. His presence at the midpoint and the ending anchors the mix in his specific emotional aesthetic: warmth filtered through technology, intimacy processed into beats. Between them, FKA twigs' "tears in the club" at 17:45 returns from Mix 06 — her R&B-art-pop arriving from entirely outside the electronic music ecosystem and finding a natural home between house tracks. The DJ's ability to integrate these outliers has grown so refined that twigs no longer feels like a digression. She feels like part of the language.

The energy arc peaks in the third quarter — the classic warm-up, peak, cool-down shape compressed into under an hour. Every section has to earn its time because there is no time to waste. The Afro-house thread deepens: DJ Tira & DJ Sox's "Single Guys" at 5:30 and Hyenah's "Ezizweni" at 23:45 (played twice) carry the South African sound that has been growing since Mix 10, now occupying significant real estate in a thirty-track session. Fireboy DML & Ed Sheeran's "Peru" at 15:00 — one of 2021's biggest Afrobeats crossovers — sits comfortably in this context.

David Morales' "Needin' U" at 45:30 is a moment of pure reverence. One of house music's all-time classics, originally released in 1998, deployed deep in a compact modern set. Michael Bibi's "PARADISE" at 47:45 — contemporary tech-house from one of the genre's current stars — follows, and the two tracks, separated by decades, share more DNA than the calendar would suggest. DJ Tennis' "Atlanta" at 44:45 provides the deep, hypnotic connective tissue between the classic and the current. The DJ understands the lineage not as history but as a living conversation.

The most remarkable thing about Mix 17 is what isn't here. No pop anthems. No rock classics. No hip-hop detours. No ambient outros. Just house music and its close relatives, for fifty-eight uninterrupted minutes. After sixteen mixes of genre exploration — from Pink Floyd to The Notorious B.I.G. to Fine Young Cannibals — the DJ has produced their most focused work, and it happens to be their shortest. That's not a coincidence. Focus and brevity are the same discipline expressed in different dimensions. When you know exactly what you want to say, you don't need long to say it.
