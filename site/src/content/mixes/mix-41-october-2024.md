## Liner Notes

The nostalgia record. Thirty-one tracks across fifty-five minutes, and nearly every one of them is a song you already know. Benson Boone's "Beautiful Things" opens the mix. Pryda's "Mirage" follows. Jack U's "Where Are U Now" with Justin Bieber at two minutes. Taylor Swift's "Don't Blame Me" at seven. Kings of Leon's "Use Somebody" at nine. The first ten minutes read less like a tracklist and more like a memory palace.

The energy carries the same quiet hum that defines the era — but the selections burn brighter than any number could suggest. These are anthems, records that carry entire summers in their first four bars. The DJ isn't trying to build a mood from scratch. They're curating a mood from decades of accumulated feeling. The technique is different from the early marathons, where the DJ's taste was still forming and popular tracks served as orientation points. By Mix 41, the popular tracks serve as emotional architecture.

BICEP's "Glue" appears at nineteen minutes in a Tomo Remix. The original "Glue" was in Mix 01 — the first mix, the beginning of everything. It appeared again in Mix 12, again in Mix 29, again in Mix 35. Each return has felt like a homecoming, and this one is no different, though the remix gives it a slightly unfamiliar cast, like hearing a friend's voice through a phone line from a country you've never visited. Bicep has been the archive's most important discovery — the gateway drug, as the arc document names them. Their presence here, six years deep, confirms that some discoveries don't fade.

RUFUS DU SOL's "Pressure" at twenty-seven minutes is placed perfectly — nearly the halfway point, the moment when the mix pivots from its pop-leaning first half to its more electronic second. deadmau5's "Strobe" follows at thirty-five minutes, one of electronic music's most universally recognised tracks, a ten-minute progressive masterpiece compressed into what sounds like a moment in the mix's dense sequencing. These are the archive's lifers, the artists who have been present since the first era and whose selections now carry the weight of all the mixes they've appeared in before.

Kasablanca's "Breathe" at thirty-nine minutes continues a thread that began when the artist first appeared in Mix 37. They've quickly become part of the new guard — artists who arrived in the quiet era and seem to understand its sensibility intuitively. Their melodic, synth-heavy approach fits the reduced energy like a glove.

The "Every Breath You Take" double at forty-nine minutes — Deep Chills' version followed by Strizzo's exclusive — is the mix's most playful moment. The Police's melody, one of the most recognisable in pop history, refracted through two different dance productions back to back. It recalls the Tiesto triptych from Mix 37 and the James Hype quadruple from Mix 40. The DJ has developed a signature technique: taking a single iconic melody and examining it through multiple lenses in quick succession.

OneRepublic's "Counting Stars" at forty-four minutes hits differently in this context. Removed from radio rotation and placed between house tracks, the song sheds its pop gloss and reveals the yearning underneath. The DJ has always had this talent — hearing what a song is really about beneath its genre dressing and placing it where that essence can breathe.

Mix 41 closes with Steve Aoki, Trinix, and Akon's "Locked Up" — the same track that appeared in Mix 36. Repetitions across adjacent mixes are rarer now than in the early years, which makes them more meaningful when they occur. The DJ is still circling certain records, still finding them necessary, still reaching for them when a session needs closing.
