## Liner Notes

There are nights when you press record not because you have something to say, but because you can't stop listening. September 2018 was one of those nights. Three hours and forty-five minutes, ninety tracks, no plan except to keep the music moving. This isn't a DJ set in any traditional sense. It's a window into the listening habits of someone who has just discovered that they can connect the songs they love, one after another, and that the connections themselves are the point.

Pyramid's "Cole's Memories" opens the archive — not with a beat but with a sweep of strings at 0:45, cinematic and wide-eyed. It's a soundtrack piece playing the role of an overture, and for those first moments the mix belongs to the movies before anything else arrives. Then Kölsch's "Grey" settles in at 5:45, grey-toned and meditative, threading that thin line between melancholy and motion that defines so much of what's to come. But the real beginning — the one that will echo through thirteen more mixes and five more years — arrives at 7:45. Bicep's "Glue." The cascading melody, the rave nostalgia, the way it sounds like remembering something you haven't experienced yet. This is the track that opened a door. Everything that follows walks through it.

The first hour is a map of a music collection without borders. Drake's "God's Plan" in David Puentez's edit at 22:00 sits between Kölsch's brooding techno and Phil K's deep electronica. Calvin Harris and Sam Smith's "Promises" at 27:30 shares air with Selena Gomez's "Back To You." Bag Raiders' "Shooting Stars" at 35:45 gives way to Moloko's "Sing It Back" in Herbert's Tasteful Dub at 43:15 — a 1999 UK house classic arriving as though it had always been part of the furniture. There's no genre anxiety anywhere in this hour. No sense that pop and house and techno need separate rooms. It's the unfiltered taste of someone who hasn't yet learned to edit, and there's a rawness to that honesty which the more refined mixes ahead will never quite recapture.

Four RÜFÜS DU SOL tracks cluster around the one-hour mark: "Desert Night" at 1:00:15, its Jesse Rose Remix three minutes later, "Innerbloom" at 1:09:15, and "Underwater" at 1:21:45. This isn't careful curation — it's an obsession declaring itself in real time. RÜFÜS DU SOL will appear in fourteen mixes across every single era of this archive, the one artist who never leaves, never fades, never gets replaced. But you can hear the attachment forming here, in this concentration of yearning Australian electronic music that stretches the middle of the mix into something warm and wide and aching. The way "Innerbloom" unfolds over those minutes — patient, building, trusting you to stay — tells you something about the kind of DJ this person will become.

Between the RÜFÜS DU SOL cluster and the two-hour mark, the mix reveals its other founding relationships. Mood II Swing's "Closer" arrives at 49:15 in the Deepshakerz Rework, all soulful vocal and deep house pulse — a track that will anchor six more mixes whenever the selection needs grounding. Lane 8's "Fingerprint" at 1:05:30 is a quiet introduction to an artist who'll stay for eight mixes across five years, melodic and patient, trusting the listener to lean in. And at 1:44:00, deadmau5's "Strobe" appears in Lane 8's remix — two of the archive's most enduring artists connected through a single reimagining of perhaps the most beloved progressive house track of a generation. These are the threads that will carry forward long after the marathon era ends.

The two-hour mark unlocks something looser and more personal. Lane 8's "Loving You" appears at 2:03:45, and two minutes later, Solee's remix of the same track arrives. It's a small moment that sets up one of the archive's longest-running threads: Solee's "Shanti" debuts at 2:13:00, a hypnotic deep house piece that will span the entire seven-year journey from this first mix to the forty-sixth and final chapter. Listen to how the mix finds "Shanti" through "Loving You" — one artist's remix leading naturally to another artist's original, a discovery rather than a decision. Both become permanent residents of the collection. Both were found, not chosen.

From the midpoint onward, the mix becomes a celebration of everything this person loves without apology. Justice's "D.A.N.C.E." in the MSTRKRFT Remix at 2:17:00. Whitney Houston's "I Wanna Dance with Somebody" at 2:22:00. Sugababes' "Push The Button" at 2:26:00. Michael Jackson's "Rock With You" at 2:58:30. MGMT's "Kids" in the Soulwax Remix at 2:41:45, and then "Electric Feel" in the Justice Remix at 3:06:45. These aren't ironic inclusions or calculated crowd-pleasers — they're the records this person actually dances to when nobody's watching, and their presence here gives the final ninety minutes a warmth and generosity that genre-pure mixes never achieve. Drake returns with "In My Feelings" at 3:15:15 and "Nonstop" at 1:17:00. Red Hot Chili Peppers' "By the Way" appears at 2:53:30. The jukebox is open, and everything is welcome.

The energy holds remarkably steady throughout — no dramatic builds, no calculated drops, no peak-hour crescendo. The mix hums at roughly the same intensity from its first quarter through its last, the sonic signature of someone mixing for themselves rather than engineering a room. That steady warmth carries you through nearly four hours the way a long night drive carries you through unfamiliar country: you look up and realise you've been moving for hours, and you can't quite say when you stopped noticing the road.

Robin Schulz's "Sun Goes Down" appears twice to close — the radio mix at 3:42:00 and ManiezzL's remix at 3:45:00 — as though the mix can't quite bring itself to end. The sun literally goes down on an entire evening compressed into a single recording. Everything that will define the next forty-five chapters is already here, buried in the sprawl: the deep house foundations in Mood II Swing and Solee, the melodic electronic core in Lane 8 and deadmau5, the pop fluency, the RÜFÜS DU SOL devotion, Bicep's "Glue" as an opening statement, "Shanti" as a quiet constant. The discipline and the editing and the quiet turn — all of that is still years away. But the heart was here first, in this overstuffed, generous, slightly naive first chapter. It's always been here.
