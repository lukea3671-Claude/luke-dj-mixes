## Liner Notes

The hottest mix in the archive. Every quarter holds above the average of most other mixes, and the opening section pushes higher than any single quarter across all forty-six chapters. Seventy-five minutes, forty-nine tracks, the key shifted to F — and the DJ has woken up with something to prove.

The opening five minutes are a masterclass in density and intent. Jamie Jones' "My Paradise" at 0:00, Joeski & Gorge's "Jogo" at 2:15, SolyMar & Megamen's "All I Need" at 3:15, and then Andy Compton's "That Acid Track" at 3:30 followed immediately by Solee's "Shanti" at 4:30 — two of the archive's most cherished signatures deployed back to back within the first five minutes. "That Acid Track" again at 5:45. Three signature tracks in two minutes. It's like a DJ set that opens with a greatest-hits invocation of its own mythology, a reminder of everything the archive has been building toward, played as though the DJ needs to hear it all at once before the real work begins.

Bicep's "Glue" returns at 19:15 in a Hugo Cantarra & Stone Van Brooken edit — the track's fourth incarnation across the archive, each version reflecting a different relationship. The Tomorrowland version in Mix 01 was discovery. The original in Mix 17 was memory. This edit is reinterpretation: the same melody refracted through new hands, proving that the song is durable enough to survive transformation. David Morales' "Needin' U" at 16:30 places "Glue" in historical company — one of house music's all-time classics sitting minutes away from one of electronic music's defining gateway tracks.

Basic Channel's "Phylyps Trak II/II" at 24:15 is now on its third appearance, embedded so naturally between LP Giobbi remixes and Epic Hunters that you might not notice the Berlin dub techno landmark slipping past. The DJ no longer needs to foreground their influences. They just deploy them — foundational documents nestled inside contemporary selections, the architecture invisible unless you know what you're hearing.

Tensnake's "Antibodies" in LP Giobbi's remix appears three times across a nine-minute stretch — at 24:00, 27:00, and 30:15. The same triple-play compulsion seen with Toni Braxton in Mix 08 and Tinlicker in Mix 09, applied here to a disco-house track that's catchier than either. The DJ can't let it go, and the archive has always honoured that impulse rather than editing it out. Some tracks demand to be heard more than once in a single sitting, and this is one of them — each play revealing a different layer, a different entry point into the groove.

The genre breakdown tells its own story: Dance, Electronic, and House account for 89% of the mix. Pop drops to a single track — Ed Sheeran's "2step" at 58:45, placed remarkably late, the only concession to the chart world in an otherwise committed dancefloor session. The crossover instinct of the early archive has been replaced by genre confidence. The DJ knows what they're playing and why, and the "why" is no longer about range. It's about depth.

The second half widens through Vintage Culture & Goodboys' "This Feeling" at 40:30, MEDUZA's "Bad Memories" remix at 40:45, Deetron's "Photon" returning at 46:00 from Mix 08, and Petar Dundov's "Around One" at 56:00 — Croatian minimal techno adding a texture that nothing else in the mix provides. &ME, Rampa & Adam Port's "Paris" in Chloé Caillet's remix at 52:00 brings Keinemusik's deep, sophisticated house into the fold. Prince Kaybee's "Tayari" at 1:00:45 extends the South African thread.

Swedish House Mafia's "Time" at 1:06:45 provides a rare stadium-scale moment in a mix that otherwise stays firmly in club territory. And then BIA & J. Cole's "LONDON" closes at 1:11:00 — the first hip-hop ending since the early archive, a reminder that the DJ's world extends beyond the dancefloor even when seventy minutes of unbroken dance music suggests otherwise. Mix 20 is the archive's most energetic statement since the marathon era, but delivered in seventy-five focused minutes instead of three and a half hours. The same impulse — to push, to sustain, to keep the temperature high — expressed with four years more economy. Proof that intensity and discipline aren't opposites. They're the same thing.
